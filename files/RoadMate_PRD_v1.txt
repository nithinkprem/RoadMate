1. Executive Summary
RoadMate is a mobility assistance platform that connects stranded vehicle owners in Kerala with the nearest available mechanics, tyre shops, battery services, fuel delivery agents, and towing operators in real time. It starts as a hyperlocal directory-plus-booking product for Calicut and is architected from day one to expand into a full two-sided marketplace: a Customer app, a Worker (mechanic) app, and an Admin/Super Admin back office.
The core insight driving this product is simple: when a vehicle breaks down, there is no single place that answers "who is nearby, open right now, and can help me in the next 15 minutes?" People currently fall back on Google search, Google Maps, WhatsApp groups, or calling around — none of which are purpose-built for an urgent, location-based, time-sensitive need.
Rather than attempting to become a full roadside-assistance insurer (like AAA) or a general services marketplace (like Urban Company) in version one, RoadMate deliberately starts narrow: a location + issue-type search that surfaces nearby help with live status, distance, ETA and price band, backed by a proprietary database that is richer than what Google Maps alone provides. Monetization, worker onboarding, live tracking, payments and AI-assisted diagnosis are sequenced into later phases once the core directory has proven demand.
This document defines the product for Phase 1 (the buildable MVP) in full detail — screens, data model, APIs, and acceptance criteria — and defines Phases 2 through 4 at a roadmap level of detail so that scope, budget and timeline decisions can be made without over-committing engineering effort before the core loop is validated.
1.1 Product in one line
A hyperlocal marketplace that finds you the nearest verified help for any vehicle breakdown in under a minute, and later lets you book, track, and pay for it — starting in Calicut, Kerala.
1.2 Why now
Two-wheeler and car ownership in Kerala continues to rise, and Calicut/Kozhikode's road and traffic density makes breakdowns a frequent, high-friction event.
Google Maps surfaces "tyre shops near me" but has no live open/closed reliability, no price transparency, no booking, and no worker-side tooling.
On-demand marketplace patterns (Uber, Swiggy, Urban Company) are now culturally familiar in Kerala, lowering the adoption cost for a similarly structured roadside product.
Cloud infrastructure (Supabase, Vercel, managed Postgres) makes it realistic for a small team to ship a production-grade MVP without managing servers.
1.3 What Phase 1 does and does not include
Phase 1 includes: location capture, issue-based search, a Google-Maps-backed + proprietary shop directory, shop detail pages, call/navigate/WhatsApp actions, ratings and reviews, and an admin dashboard to manage listings. Phase 1 explicitly excludes: in-app booking with worker acceptance, live GPS tracking, in-app payments, premium membership, and AI diagnosis — these are sequenced into Phases 2 through 4 once the directory has real usage data.

2. Vision, Mission & Problem Statement
2.1 Vision
To become the default platform that anyone in Kerala opens the moment their vehicle breaks down — and eventually, the operating layer for on-demand vehicle care across India.
2.2 Mission
Give every stranded vehicle owner a fast, transparent, trustworthy way to find nearby help, and give every independent mechanic, tyre shop, and roadside worker a digital storefront and steady stream of customers — starting with the single, well-defined use case of breakdown assistance in Calicut.
2.3 Problem Statement
When a vehicle breaks down in Kerala — a punctured tyre, a dead battery, an empty tank, a bike that won't start, a broken chain — the owner has no single, reliable channel to find nearby help. Today's alternatives are all imperfect:

2.4 Guiding Product Principle
RoadMate is built and named as a Mobility Assistance Platform, not narrowly as "Roadside Assistance." This is deliberate: the same location + issue-type + nearby-provider core loop extends naturally to tyre shops, battery services, fuel delivery, towing, EV charging, bike wash, insurance, and scheduled service booking, without requiring a re-architecture of the product later.

3. Market Research & Competitive Landscape
3.1 Market context
Kerala has one of India's highest vehicle-ownership densities relative to population, and Calicut (Kozhikode) is a dense mid-size city with heavy two-wheeler usage, making roadside breakdowns a frequent occurrence. The target launch geography is deliberately narrow — a single city — so that supply-side density (enough registered mechanics per neighbourhood) can be reached quickly, which is the precondition for the marketplace to feel reliable to a first-time user.
This document does not present sized TAM/SAM/SOM figures, since credible numbers require primary research (mechanic counts per ward, average breakdown frequency, willingness to pay) that should be gathered during Phase 1 pilot rather than assumed. This is flagged as an open research item in Section 3.4.

4.1 Why monetization is deferred
Introducing commission or subscription pricing before there is a reliable base of active shops and repeat searches risks suppressing the exact adoption the product needs to prove out. Phase 1's only goal is to prove the core loop: a user searches, finds a relevant nearby option, and takes an action (call, navigate, or WhatsApp).

6. User Journeys
6.1 Customer journey — Phase 1 (Directory & Discovery)
Opens RoadMate website/app.
Grants location access (or searches a location manually).
Selects the issue: Tyre Puncture, Battery, Mechanic, Fuel Delivery, Towing, Flat Tyre, Car Wash, or Air Filling.
Views a map with nearby matching shops, plus a ranked list of cards (rating, distance, open/closed, ETA, price range).
Opens a shop detail page for more information (services, hours, photos, reviews).
Takes an action: Call, Navigate (opens Google Maps directions), or WhatsApp.
Optionally leaves a rating/review after the interaction.
6.2 Customer journey — Phase 2+ (Booking & Tracking)
Steps 1–5 as above.
Instead of calling directly, taps "Book" and submits a job request.
Nearby available workers are notified; one accepts the job.
Customer sees the worker's live location and ETA (Phase 3).
Job is marked complete by the worker; customer pays in-app (Phase 3) and rates the job.
6.3 Worker (mechanic) journey — Phase 2+
Registers with name, phone, location, vehicle type serviced, services offered, pricing, experience, and documents.
Account is verified by the Admin team before going live.
Toggles Online/Offline availability.
Receives job requests, can Accept or Reject.
Navigates to customer location, marks job complete.
Views earnings and ratings history.
6.4 Admin journey
Logs into the Admin Panel.
Manages the shop/listing directory: add, edit, verify, deactivate.
Reviews and approves worker registrations and documents (Phase 2+).
Monitors bookings, complaints, and reviews for moderation.
Views analytics: searches by issue type, city coverage, most-viewed shops.

Requirement

11.7 Row-Level Security (RLS)
Supabase RLS policies restrict: shop owners (once self-serve onboarding exists in Phase 2) to editing only their own listing; customers to editing only their own reviews and profile; Admin role bypasses restrictions via a service-role policy scoped to the Admin panel backend only, never exposed to the client.

14.2 Key security requirements
All write endpoints require authentication; RLS policies enforce row-level ownership at the database layer, not just in application code.
Phone numbers of individual workers/owners are not exposed in public API responses beyond what's needed for the Call action (click-to-call, not raw scraping-friendly display, once workers are onboarded in Phase 2).

17.1 High-level architecture
The client (Next.js app, deployed on Vercel) talks directly to Supabase for auth, data reads/writes (governed by RLS), and storage, with a small number of Edge Functions for geo-ranked search and admin analytics aggregation that shouldn't run as raw client-side queries. Google Maps Platform is called client-side for map rendering and directions, and server-side (or via a scheduled job) for the initial shop-data seed/enrichment. This keeps Phase 1 server-less and cheap to run, while leaving a clear seam to introduce a dedicated Node.js service in Phase 2+ for booking-matching logic, notification dispatch, and payment webhook handling.
